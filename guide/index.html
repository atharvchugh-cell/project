What is a stock?

A stock is a tiny ownership slice of a real company. When you buy one share of Apple, you literally own a fraction of Apple. If Apple sells more iPhones and grows, your share becomes more valuable. If sales drop, the share is worth less. Stocks are how most people in the U.S. build long-term wealth — but they also go down, sometimes a lot, in the short term.

The price of a stock changes every second the market is open. It moves based on what buyers are willing to pay and what sellers will accept — which depends on company earnings, news, the broader economy, and plain human emotion.

The S&P 500: the easiest place to start

The S&P 500 is an index that tracks the 500 largest U.S. companies — Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, JPMorgan, Coca-Cola, and many more. Instead of trying to pick a single winner, you can buy a fund that holds all 500 at once. This is called an index fund.

Historically, the S&P 500 has returned roughly 10% per year on average over long time periods. It's not guaranteed, and there are losing years — but it's the benchmark most professional investors try (and usually fail) to beat. For beginners, an S&P 500 index fund is often the smartest first investment.

Individual stocks vs index funds

Buying an individual stock — say, just Nvidia — can pay off enormously if the company does well, but it's much riskier. A single company can have a bad quarter, lose a key customer, or get disrupted by a competitor. An index fund spreads that risk across hundreds of companies, so no single failure can wipe you out.

A common beginner strategy: put most of your money in an index fund, then pick one or two individual stocks you genuinely believe in. Our simulator lets you try both approaches side-by-side, with real historical price data, so you can see the tradeoffs without risking a dollar.

Gold, oil, and other commodities

Commodities are physical things — gold bars, barrels of crude oil, bushels of wheat. Investors buy them as a hedge: gold often holds value when stocks crash, and oil prices respond to global events. They don't produce earnings the way a company does, so they're usually a small slice of a portfolio, not the foundation.

CDs and high-yield savings

A certificate of deposit (CD) is a deal with a bank: you lock up your money for 1, 2, or 5 years and the bank pays you a fixed interest rate. You can't touch the money early without a penalty, but the return is guaranteed. A high-yield savings account (HYSA) pays a smaller variable rate but lets you withdraw anytime.

These are the safe portion of a portfolio. They won't make you rich, but they won't lose value either — useful for money you'll need soon, or as a calm anchor when stocks are volatile.

The three rules every investor learns

  1. Diversify. Don't put everything in one stock. Spread your money across many companies, and across different asset types.
  2. Think long-term. The market goes up and down every day. The people who win are the ones who keep buying through both, and hold for years.
  3. Only invest money you don't need soon. If you might need it in the next year or two, keep it in savings — not stocks.

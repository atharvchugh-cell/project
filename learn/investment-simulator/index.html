What is an investment simulator?

An investment simulator is a program that lets you buy and sell assets with virtual money and keeps score as real market prices move. Nothing is at stake financially — which is exactly why it is the cheapest place to make the mistakes that everyone makes once.

Published by Rise to Riches · Last updated 15 August 2026

Live paper trading vs. historical replay

Simulators come in two shapes, and the difference matters more than the interface.

Live paper trading mirrors today's market in real time. You place an order, and it fills at roughly the current quote. It is realistic, but it runs at the speed of real life: a market cycle takes years, so a semester of paper trading mostly teaches you what one flat quarter feels like.

Historical replay — what this site does — starts you at a real past year and fast forwards through actual recorded returns. Twenty years of market history compresses into about twenty minutes, so a single session contains a bubble, a crash, and a recovery. You get to feel the part of investing that only shows up over decades: that the worst year and the best year are usually close together, and that most of the damage is self-inflicted during the gap.

The trade-off is honest: replay cannot teach you today's news cycle, and if you already know what happened in 2008 you can cheat. That is why the years are hidden by default in classroom games.

What makes a simulator worth your time

  • Real data, not invented prices. Randomly generated charts teach pattern superstition. Recorded returns teach history. Ours are documented on the methodology page.
  • More than one asset class. If the only choice is which stock, you never learn allocation. Stocks, an index fund, gold, oil, CDs and a savings account behave differently in the same year — that disagreement is the lesson in how diversification works.
  • A long enough horizon. Anything under about ten simulated years is a trading game, not an investing one.
  • A scoreboard that measures the right thing. Ending value alone rewards a lucky single-stock bet. Comparing your return against buying the S&P 500 and holding it is the comparison that actually tells you something.
  • No account required. A simulator that wants your email before your first trade is a lead form.

A practice routine that teaches something

  1. Run one game on instinct. Trade however you want. Write down your ending value and your return versus the index.
  2. Run a second game as a pure index buyer. Put everything in the S&P 500 fund on day one and touch nothing. Many first-time players lose to this run, and finding that out costs nothing here.
  3. Run a third with a written rule. For example: 70% index, 20% individual stocks, 10% cash, rebalanced whenever a slice drifts past 10 points. Following a rule you wrote in advance is the whole skill.
  4. Compare the three. The gap between game one and game three is a fair estimate of how much your instincts cost you.

If you want to test a specific "what if" — a crash two years after you start, a decade of high inflation — the Sandbox simulator lets you place those events on a timeline and re-run the same portfolio against them.

What a simulator can't teach

Virtual money does not hurt. Selling a real position down 40% is a different experience from clicking sell on a number, and no simulator closes that gap. Simulators also tend to ignore taxes, bid-ask spreads, and the fact that you cannot always buy exactly what you want at exactly the closing price.

Treat the result as a lesson about behaviour and allocation, not as a forecast, and definitely not as evidence that a strategy will work with real money. This site is an educational simulation and is not financial advice.
